# Lesson video scripts

One script for every lesson video: 11 lessons, three levels each, 33 videos in
all. Hand this file to the interpreter who records them.

Each script follows its lesson's story, summary and quiz in
`content/modules/`, so the video teaches what the quiz then asks about.

## Notes for the interpreter

- **Short sentences, everyday words.** Each line is meant to be said (and
  signed) as written. If a word feels stiff, change it to how you would
  naturally say it, as long as the meaning and the numbers stay the same.
- **Numbers matter.** Say every amount slowly and clearly - "five hundred
  rupees", not "five hundred". The quizzes check these exact sums.
- **Lines in `[square brackets]` are not spoken.** They tell the editor what
  to show on screen at that point (a picture, a sum, a word).
- **Pauses.** A blank line between paragraphs means a short pause. Let the
  viewer catch up, especially after a sum.
- **Length.** Each script is roughly the length on the lesson card when said at
  a calm pace. Going a little over or under is fine.
- **Keep figures current.** Lessons 10 and 11 quote government amounts
  (tax deductions, income limits). These come from `content/schemes.json`.
  Check them against that file on the day you record, in case they changed.

After recording, the clip goes in `video.signInterpreter.src` for that level -
see "Swapping in the real videos" in the main README. The scripts can also be
used as the starting text for each level's caption file.

## Lesson 1 - What is Money?

### Level 1 - Why money works at all (about 4 minutes)

Hello! Today we ask a simple question. What is money, and why does it work?

Let us start with a story. It is an old Jataka tale, called "The old bowl".

[Show: a black, dirty bowl]

A poor family had an old bowl. It was black with soot. Nobody looked at it.
But the bowl was made of solid gold. The family did not know.

A trader came to the door. He scratched the bowl and saw the gold. But he
said, "This is worthless. I will give you a few coins for it." The family said
no.

Later, a second trader came. He also saw the gold. But he told the truth. He
said, "This bowl is worth more than everything I have." He gave them all his
money and took the bowl.

The first trader came back that evening. The bowl was gone.

Both traders knew the price. Only one was honest. The family's problem was
simple: they did not know what their bowl was worth. Knowing the price
protects you.

Now, why do we need money at all?

[Show: a farmer with rice, a tailor with cloth]

Long ago there was no money. People swapped things. This is called barter.

A farmer has rice. The farmer wants cloth. So the farmer must find a person who
has cloth, and who also wants rice. That is two matches. That is hard.

Money removes the second match. The farmer sells rice to anyone. Then the
farmer buys cloth from anyone. Easy.

For this to work, money must be three things. Hard to copy. Easy to carry. Easy
to count.

[Show: a rupee note with "Reserve Bank of India" highlighted]

In India, only the Reserve Bank of India can print rupee notes. If anyone could
print notes, there would be too many. Nobody would trust them.

Today, a lot of money has no paper at all. When you pay by UPI, no note moves.
Your account goes down by two hundred rupees. The shop's account goes up by two
hundred rupees. Only two numbers change.

[Show: phone screens, minus ₹200 and plus ₹200]

Let us remember. Money replaced swapping. Money must be hard to copy, easy to
carry, and easy to count. Only the Reserve Bank of India prints rupees. And UPI
just changes numbers between accounts.

Now try the quiz. Good luck!

### Level 2 - What gives money its value (about 5 minutes)

Hello! Today's question is: why is a piece of paper worth five hundred rupees?

First, a story. An old Jataka tale, "The old bowl".

[Show: two traders on a road]

Two traders came to the same town on the same day. In one house was an old
black bowl. The family thought it was worth nothing. It was really solid gold.

The first trader tested it and knew. But he said, "It is worthless," and
offered a few coins. He planned to sell it for a fortune. The family said no.
He walked away. He thought they would say yes later, when they needed money.

The second trader tested the same bowl. He said out loud, "This is gold." He
gave them every coin and every good in his cart. He kept only his boat fare.

The first trader came back to an empty house.

Both men knew the same thing. The difference was whether they told the family.
This is called an information gap. You close it by finding out what a thing is
worth before you agree a price.

Now, about money itself.

[Show: a ₹500 note]

A five hundred rupee note costs only a few rupees to make. So where does the
rest of the value come from? Trust. Everyone accepts it, because everyone else
accepts it. If people stopped trusting it, the paper would be useless.

Money does three jobs.

[Show: three icons - a shop, a box, a ruler]

One: it pays for things now. Two: it stores value for later. Three: it measures
what things are worth.

The third job is why we have prices. Without money, you could not say "a
bicycle is worth about two hundred snacks".

Now, the second job - storing value - has a problem. It is called inflation.
Inflation means prices go up over time.

[Show: ₹100 → ₹94]

If prices rise six percent a year, one hundred rupees in a drawer will buy only
about ninety-four rupees of things next year. After ten years, it buys about
half. The note does not change. What it can buy changes.

So saving is not just putting notes in a box. The rate your money earns matters
as much as the number you see.

Let us remember. Money's value is trust. Money pays, stores, and measures.
Inflation makes stored money buy less each year.

Now try the quiz!

### Level 3 - Who decides how much money there is (about 5 minutes)

Hello! Today we ask: who decides how much money a country has?

[Show: Reserve Bank of India building or logo]

In India, the answer is the Reserve Bank of India - the RBI. It is the
country's central bank. It issues every rupee note.

Can the RBI just print more money and make everyone rich? No. Here is why.

[Show: a pile of money growing, a pile of goods staying the same]

Imagine a country prints twice as much money. But farms and factories still
make the same amount of goods. Now there is more money chasing the same goods.
So prices go up. That rise in prices is called inflation.

The RBI tries to keep inflation near four percent a year. Its main tool is the
repo rate. The repo rate is the interest the RBI charges banks when they borrow
from it.

Now, two important words. Nominal value and real value.

[Show: "Nominal = the number" and "Real = what it buys"]

Nominal value is the number written on the note. Real value is what the note
can actually buy.

Here is an example. Your pocket money goes up from five hundred to five hundred
and twenty-five rupees. That is five percent more. Good news? But prices went
up eight percent in the same year. So you can actually buy less than before.
The number went up. The real value went down.

The same is true for a salary. A salary that rises five percent while prices
rise seven percent has gone down in real value.

One more idea. The rupee has a price against other countries' money. This is
called the exchange rate.

[Show: rupee and US dollar, with a phone and a fuel pump]

When the rupee gets weaker, things we buy from other countries cost more. Phones
and fuel are good examples.

Let us remember. The RBI issues rupees and tries to keep inflation near four
percent. More money with the same goods means higher prices. Look at real value,
not just the number. And a weak rupee makes imported things cost more.

Now try the quiz!

## Lesson 2 - Saving vs Spending

### Level 1 - Choosing to save or to spend (about 4 minutes)

Hello! Today we talk about a choice you make every day. Save, or spend?

Here is the first rule. Every rupee you spend is a rupee you cannot save.

So how do we save well? We make a savings goal.

A savings goal is something you are saving for. It has a price, and it has a
date.

[Show: a bicycle with a ₹4000 price tag]

Write the goal down. For example: a bicycle costs four thousand rupees. You
save two hundred rupees every month.

How long will it take? Four thousand divided by two hundred is twenty. So it
takes twenty months.

[Show: ₹4000 ÷ ₹200 = 20 months]

What if you already have some money saved? Take it away from the price first,
then divide. Always count what you already have.

Now, a warning. Small spends add up.

[Show: a snack, then a calendar]

Twenty rupees a day does not feel like much. But in one month, that is six
hundred rupees. That is a lot closer to your bicycle!

So before you buy small things every day, stop and think. Is this worth more to
me than my goal?

Let us remember. Spending and saving use the same rupees. A savings goal has a
price and a date. Divide the price by what you save each month. And small daily
spends become big monthly spends.

Now try the quiz!

### Level 2 - Saving, spending and opportunity cost (about 5 minutes)

Hello! Today we learn a big idea with a big name: opportunity cost.

Opportunity cost is what you give up when you choose.

[Show: headphones on one side, a game on the other]

Say you have five hundred rupees. You can buy headphones or a game. Not both.
If you buy the headphones, the opportunity cost is the game you did not buy.

Every choice has an opportunity cost. Even saving has one - it is the thing you
did not buy today.

Now, a helpful habit. It is called "pay yourself first".

Pay yourself first means you save before you spend. Not after. Before.

Most people spend first and save what is left. Usually, nothing is left. So
flip it around.

[Show: ₹100 split into ₹20 and ₹80]

A common plan is to save twenty rupees out of every hundred you get. That is
twenty percent.

So if you get one thousand rupees, you put two hundred rupees away first. Then
you spend from the eight hundred that is left.

[Show: ₹1000 - ₹200 saved = ₹800 to spend]

One more thing: an emergency fund.

An emergency fund is money you keep only for surprise problems. A broken phone.
A doctor's bill. A school trip you did not know about.

It is not for wants. It is for real surprises. When you use it, fill it up
again.

Let us remember. Opportunity cost is what you give up. Pay yourself first:
save before you spend. Try saving twenty out of every hundred rupees. And keep
an emergency fund for surprises only.

Now try the quiz!

### Level 3 - Saving with a goal and a date (about 5 minutes)

Hello! Today we learn how to turn a wish into a plan.

"Save more" is a wish. "Three thousand rupees by Diwali" is a plan. A plan has a
goal, an amount, and a date.

[Show: "₹3,000 by Diwali" on a calendar]

How do you reach it? Divide the goal by the months you have. Three thousand
rupees in six months is five hundred rupees a month.

[Show: ₹3,000 ÷ 6 = ₹500 a month]

Now, some costs come once a year. School fees. A festival. A birthday.

They always arrive at the same time, but they still feel like a shock. Why?
Because we did not plan.

The answer is a sinking fund. A sinking fund means you save a little every month
for a big cost that comes later.

[Show: ₹6,000 school fees in June, split into 12 jars of ₹500]

School fees are six thousand rupees every June. Save five hundred rupees a month
for twelve months. When June comes, the money is ready.

Next, the emergency fund. A good emergency fund covers three to six months of
basic costs. Not just one surprise. That way, a hard few months will not break
you.

Now watch out for something called lifestyle creep.

[Show: income going up, spending going up with it]

Lifestyle creep is when you spend more every time you earn more. Your income
grows, but your savings never do. The fix: when you get more, raise your saving
first.

Last idea. Waiting for a reward is hard. Everyone finds it hard. A plan with a
date helps, because you can see the finish line.

Let us remember. A plan has a goal, an amount, and a date. Divide to find the
monthly amount. Use a sinking fund for yearly costs. Keep three to six months in
an emergency fund. And when income rises, raise your saving first.

Now try the quiz!

## Lesson 3 - Needs vs Wants

### Level 1 - Sorting needs from wants (about 4 minutes)

Hello! Today we learn to sort things into two groups. Needs, and wants.

A need is something you must have. A want is something nice to have, but you
can live without it.

But it is not always simple.

Some things are a need for one person and a want for another.

[Show: a bus pass]

A bus pass is a need if you must take the bus to school. But if you live close
and can walk, it may be a want.

The same thing can also be both.

[Show: one pair of school shoes, then many pairs]

School shoes are a need. A tenth pair of shoes is a want.

So how do you decide? Ask two questions.

[Show the two questions on screen]

Question one: what happens if I do not buy this?

Question two: can I wait?

If nothing bad happens, and you can wait, it is probably a want.

One more thing. Watch out for advertisements.

[Show: an advert saying "MUST HAVE!"]

Advertisements try to make wants feel like needs. They say "must have" and "buy
now". But you decide what you need - not the advert.

Let us remember. Needs are must-haves. Wants are nice-to-haves. The same thing
can be different for different people. Ask: what happens if I do not buy it,
and can I wait? And do not let adverts decide for you.

Now try the quiz!

### Level 2 - Needs, wants and spending priorities (about 5 minutes)

Hello! Today we learn how adults sort their spending.

Adults sort costs into two groups. Fixed costs and flexible costs.

[Show: "Fixed" with a house and school; "Flexible" with snacks and a cinema]

Fixed costs stay the same each month. Rent is a fixed cost. School fees are a
fixed cost. You must pay them.

Flexible costs change. You control them. Snacks are flexible. Outings are
flexible.

Why does this matter? Because when money is short, you cut flexible costs first.

[Show: crossing out "weekend outing", keeping "school fees" and "medicine"]

If money is short this month, do you stop paying school fees? No. Do you stop
buying medicine? No. You cut the weekend outing.

Also, plan the fixed costs first. If rent is five thousand rupees, put that
aside at the start of the month. Then spend what is left. If you spend on fun
first, you may not have enough for rent.

Here is a helpful trick. The twenty-four hour rule.

[Show: a clock, "24 hours"]

Before you buy a want that costs more than five hundred rupees, wait one day.
Just one day. Many times, you will find you do not really want it any more.

Buying without thinking is called impulse buying. The twenty-four hour rule
stops it.

Let us remember. Fixed costs stay the same. Flexible costs you control. Pay
fixed costs first. When money is short, cut flexible costs. And wait twenty-four
hours before a big want.

Now try the quiz!

### Level 3 - How sellers get you to spend (about 5 minutes)

Hello! Today we learn some tricks that sellers use to make you spend more.

Sellers study how people make choices. Then they design offers to make you buy
more. Let us look at some of them.

Trick one: the crossed-out price.

[Show: "~~₹2,000~~ ₹999"]

A shop shows "two thousand rupees" crossed out, and "nine hundred ninety-nine"
next to it. It looks like a huge discount. But was it ever really two thousand?
Compare the price with other shops, not with the crossed-out number.

Trick two: "buy now, pay later".

[Show: Phone A ₹12,000 vs Phone B ₹1,100 × 12]

Small monthly payments make a big price feel small. Phone A costs twelve
thousand rupees once. Phone B is "only eleven hundred a month" for twelve
months. Add it up: eleven hundred times twelve is thirteen thousand two hundred.
Phone B costs more. Always add every payment.

Trick three: subscriptions.

A subscription takes a little money every month. It is easy to forget. But
twelve small payments make one large payment. A one-hundred-fifty rupee app
costs one thousand eight hundred rupees in a year.

Now, a smarter test. Cost per use.

[Show: shoes, ₹1,000 ÷ 200 = ₹5]

A pair of shoes costs one thousand rupees. You wear them two hundred times. That
is five rupees each time. Something cheap that you use once can cost more per
use than something good that you use every day.

Last trick: friends.

When all your friends buy something, it feels like a need. Ask yourself: would I
still want this if nobody else had it?

Let us remember. Ignore crossed-out prices and compare shops. Add up every EMI
payment. Count subscriptions for a whole year. Think about cost per use. And
check if you really want it, or if your friends do.

Now try the quiz!

## Lesson 4 - Simple Budgeting

### Level 1 - Building your first budget (about 4 minutes)

Hello! Today you will build your first budget. But first, a story from the
Panchatantra. It is called "The pot of flour".

[Show: a pot hanging over a bed]

A man was given a pot of flour. He hung it on a hook above his bed and lay down
under it.

Then he started to dream. "I will sell the flour and buy a goat. The goat will
have babies. I will sell them and buy a cow. The cow will give milk. I will sell
the milk and buy more cows!"

In his dream, he had a big farm and a big house. He was so happy, he kicked his
legs. His foot hit the pot. It fell and broke. All the flour was gone. And so
was his plan.

His plan was not impossible. His mistake was counting money he did not have yet.
A budget uses only money you really have.

So, what is a budget?

[Show: two columns, "Money in" and "Money out"]

A budget has two sides. Money in, and money out.

Money in is called income. Pocket money is income. Money you earn helping a
neighbour is income.

Money out is called expenses. Snacks are expenses. Bus fare is an expense.

Now the sum. Income minus expenses is what you have left.

[Show: ₹300 - ₹280 = ₹20]

If your income is three hundred rupees and your expenses are two hundred eighty,
you have twenty rupees left. Good.

But what if the answer is below zero? Then your plan does not work. You must
change it before you spend. Spend less, or find more income.

Let us remember. A budget has money in and money out. Money in is income. Money
out is expenses. Income minus expenses is what is left. If it is below zero,
change the plan. And only count money you really have.

Now try the quiz!

### Level 2 - Budgeting with the 50-30-20 plan (about 5 minutes)

Hello! Today we learn a simple plan for sharing out money: the fifty-thirty-
twenty plan. But first, a story from the Panchatantra, "The pot of flour".

[Show: a pot hanging over a bed]

A man got a pot of flour. He hung it above his bed and lay down to think. "Sell
the flour, buy a goat. Sell the baby goats, buy a cow. Sell the milk, buy more
cows."

Each step made sense on its own. But he acted as if every step had already
happened. Deep in his dream, he kicked out his legs - and broke the pot. He was
left with nothing.

Every step needed the step before to work. That is why a budget keeps two lists:
money that has arrived, and money you expect. You can only spend the first one.

Now, the fifty-thirty-twenty plan.

[Show: a circle split 50 / 30 / 20]

It splits your income into three parts. Half - fifty percent - goes to needs.
Thirty percent goes to wants. Twenty percent goes to savings.

[Show: ₹1000 → ₹500 needs, ₹300 wants, ₹200 savings]

With one thousand rupees, that is five hundred for needs, three hundred for
wants, and two hundred for savings.

But a plan on paper is only the start. Next, track what you really spend. Do it
for one whole month. Write down every rupee.

Then compare. Did you spend what you planned? Maybe you planned to save two
hundred, but you only saved eighty. That is okay. Now you know. Look at where the
money went, and change something next month.

The split is a guide, not a rule. Some families need more than half for needs.
Change the plan to fit your life.

Let us remember. Fifty percent for needs, thirty for wants, twenty for savings.
Track your real spending for a month. Compare it with the plan. Then adjust.

Now try the quiz!

### Level 3 - Budgeting when income changes (about 5 minutes)

Hello! Today we learn how to budget when your income is different every month.

Many families do not earn the same amount each month. Farmers, shop workers,
tailors and gig workers can have good months and slow months.

[Show: a bar chart going up and down]

So how do you plan? Build your budget on your lowest normal month. Not your best
month.

[Show: tailor - ₹9,000 slow month, ₹15,000 busy month]

A tailor earns nine thousand rupees in a slow month and up to fifteen thousand
in a busy month. The budget should be built on nine thousand. Then the plan
always works.

What about the extra in a good month? Put it in a buffer. A buffer is money kept
aside to fill the gap in a thin month.

Good month: fill the buffer. Thin month: use the buffer.

Next, a strong method called zero-based budgeting.

[Show: every rupee with a label]

Zero-based budgeting means every rupee gets a job before the month starts. Needs,
savings, wants. You keep going until nothing is left without a job. If you have
four thousand rupees and planned three thousand six hundred, you still have four
hundred to plan.

Also, remember yearly costs. School fees. Festivals. Split them into small
monthly set-asides, so they never arrive as a shock.

Last step. At the end of each month, compare your plan with what really
happened. The difference is where you learn.

Let us remember. Plan on your lowest normal month. Put extra into a buffer. Give
every rupee a job. Set aside money for yearly costs. And compare plan and real
life every month.

Now try the quiz!

## Lesson 5 - Why Open a Bank Account

### Level 1 - Opening an account in your own name (about 4 minutes)

Hello! Today we talk about having your own bank account. First, a story from the
Panchatantra, "The mice that ate the iron".

[Show: a trader handing a heavy iron scale to a friend]

A trader was going on a long journey. He left his heavy iron scale with a friend.
They did not write anything down, because they were friends.

When he came back, the friend said, "Sorry. Mice ate the iron."

The trader did not argue. A few days later, he took the friend's son to the
river, and came back alone. "A hawk carried him away," he said.

The friend shouted, "A hawk cannot carry a boy!"

The trader said, "In a town where mice eat iron, a hawk can carry a child."

Both stories were silly. But nobody could prove anything, because there was no
record. A bank account fixes this. It writes down the amount, the date, and
whose name it is in.

Now, the good news.

[Show: a child with a bank passbook]

You can have a bank account in your own name from age ten. An adult helps manage
it until you are eighteen. But the account is yours.

A basic account needs no minimum balance. You can open it with zero rupees. If it
stays empty, it costs you nothing.

It comes with a debit card. You can pay and take out money without asking anyone
for permission.

Your account is where your own money lives - pocket money, birthday money, and
money you earn later. If your money is in someone else's account, you have to
ask them every time you want it.

One last tip. Rules can change, and different banks ask for different papers. So
check with the bank before you go.

Let us remember. A bank account is a record no one can argue with. You can open
one from age ten. A basic account needs no minimum balance. It comes with a
debit card. And your money should be in your name.

Now try the quiz!

### Level 2 - The account as a foundation, and your right to one (about 5 minutes)

Hello! Today we learn why your own bank account is so important - and why it is
your right. First, a story from the Panchatantra, "The mice that ate the iron".

[Show: a trader and a friend, an iron scale between them]

A trader left his valuable iron scale with a friend. Nothing was written down.
When he came back, the friend said sadly, "Mice ate the iron."

The trader did not argue. Instead, he took the friend's son to the river, came
back alone, and said, "A hawk carried him away." The friend was angry and
shouted that this was impossible. And that was exactly the trader's point.

Neither man could prove anything. A bank is not just a safe. A bank keeps a
record, in your name, that nobody can secretly change.

Now, why does your own account matter so much?

[Show: an account in the middle, with arrows from salary, scholarship, pension]

The account is not the goal. It is what everything else plugs into. A salary. A
scholarship. A pension. All of these are paid into an account.

And usually, money you are owed goes only into an account in your own name.
Without one, somebody else receives your money for you. That is the difference
between managing your money and being managed.

One warning. A savings account pays about four percent a year. But prices rise
about six percent. So money left there slowly loses value. An account is for
safety and easy access - not for growing money.

[Show: "RPwD Act 2016"]

Now, your rights. The Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act, 2016 says service
providers, including banks, must make their services accessible to you.

If a bank refuses to open your account, or says a hearing relative must speak
for you, that is not following the rules.

[Show: three steps - Branch manager → Grievance officer → RBI ombudsman]

Here is what to do, in order. First, ask for the branch manager. Next, the
bank's grievance officer. Then, the RBI ombudsman.

Let us remember. Your account is what everything plugs into. Money you are owed
should come to your own name. A savings account keeps money safe, but does not
grow it. And the law says banks must serve you.

Now try the quiz!

### Level 3 - How banks earn, and how your money is protected (about 5 minutes)

Hello! Today we look inside a bank. How does it make money? And how is your money
kept safe?

First, a surprise. A bank does not keep your money in a box.

[Show: money flowing from savers, through the bank, to borrowers]

The bank lends most of it to other people and businesses. It pays you a low rate
on your savings. It charges borrowers a higher rate on loans.

[Show: savers 4%, borrowers 10%, gap = bank's profit]

Say the bank pays savers four percent and charges borrowers ten percent. The gap
between them is how the bank earns.

But what if the bank fails? Your money is protected.

Deposits are insured by DICGC. It is a body under the RBI. It protects up to five
lakh rupees per person in each bank. If you had seven lakh in one bank and it
failed, five lakh would be protected.

[Show: "Insured up to ₹5 lakh"]

Now, two other ways to save at a bank.

A fixed deposit locks your money for a set time, for a better rate.

A recurring deposit takes a fixed amount from you every month. For example, five
hundred rupees every month for two years. It is good for steady saving.

Next, KYC. KYC means "Know Your Customer". It is how a bank checks who you are,
usually with Aadhaar and PAN. It stops someone else opening an account in your
name.

[Show: a lock, "PIN, OTP, password"]

And the most important rule. Never share your PIN, your OTP, or your password.
Not with anyone. Not even someone who says they are from the bank. A real bank
will never ask.

Let us remember. Banks lend your money and earn from the gap in rates. Deposits
are insured up to five lakh rupees per bank. Fixed deposits lock money; recurring
deposits save every month. KYC protects your identity. And never share your PIN,
OTP or password.

Now try the quiz!

## Lesson 6 - Earning Money

### Level 1 - Ways people earn (about 4 minutes)

Hello! Today we learn about the different ways people earn money.

The first way is a salary.

[Show: a calendar with the same amount each month]

A salary is a fixed amount paid every month. It is the same each month, even if
the month has more or fewer working days.

The second way is a wage.

A wage is paid for each hour or each day you work. If you work more days, you
earn more. If you miss a day, you earn less.

The third way is running a business. A business owner earns profit.

[Show: ₹800 sales - ₹300 costs = ₹500 profit]

Profit is sales minus costs. Say you sell twenty handmade cards for forty rupees
each. That is eight hundred rupees of sales. The materials cost three hundred
rupees. Your profit is five hundred rupees.

Now, how can you earn more? Skills.

Skills raise your pay. A worker with a special skill or licence is usually paid
more than someone untrained. Learning things is one of the best ways to earn
more.

[Show: Deaf adults working - designer, teacher, computer programmer]

And remember this. Deaf adults work in every field. Design. Teaching. Computers.
And much more. Your future is open.

Let us remember. A salary is the same every month. A wage is paid per hour or per
day. Profit is sales minus costs. Skills raise your pay. And Deaf adults work in
every field.

Now try the quiz!

### Level 2 - Earning, taxes and take-home pay (about 5 minutes)

Hello! Today we learn why the money you earn is not always the money you get.

There are two words to know. Gross pay and net pay.

[Show: "Gross = full amount", "Net = what reaches your account"]

Gross pay is the full amount you earn. Net pay is what actually reaches your
account.

The difference is called deductions. Two common deductions are income tax and
provident fund.

[Show: ₹25,000 - ₹2,000 tax - ₹1,500 PF = ₹21,500]

Say your gross pay is twenty-five thousand rupees. Income tax takes two thousand.
Provident fund takes fifteen hundred. Your net pay is twenty-one thousand five
hundred rupees.

Where does tax money go? It pays for roads, hospitals, and government schools.
Things we all use.

Now, not everyone has a salary. Some people do freelance work.

Freelance means you are paid for each project. There is no fixed salary. One
month you might earn thirty-five thousand rupees. The next month, only five
thousand. A salaried worker can rely on the same amount. A freelancer must plan
for ups and downs.

Here is an important tip. Before you accept a job, always ask: "What is the net
amount?" If a job says "salary thirty thousand rupees", ask if that is gross or
net. Otherwise you may get less than you expected.

Let us remember. Gross pay is the full amount. Net pay is what you get. The
difference is deductions, like tax and provident fund. Freelancers are paid per
project. And always ask for the net amount.

### Level 3 - How tax works, and what a job really pays (about 5 minutes)

Hello! Today we learn how income tax works, and how to compare jobs properly.

In India, income tax is charged in slabs.

[Show: income as a staircase of slices, each with its own rate]

Think of your income as slices. Each slice has its own tax rate. The rates go up
as income goes up.

Here is the key idea. A higher rate applies only to the slice above the line.
Not to all your income.

[Show: made-up example - first ₹3 lakh at 0%, the rest at 10%]

Here is a made-up example. The first three lakh rupees are taxed at zero
percent. Everything above that is taxed at ten percent. Riya earns five lakh
rupees. Only the top two lakh is taxed at ten percent. So her tax is twenty
thousand rupees.

This means a pay rise can never leave you with less money. Some people say "do
not take a raise, you will move into a higher slab". That is not true.

Your average tax rate is your total tax divided by your total income. It is
always lower than the rate on your top slab.

Now, provident fund, or PF. PF keeps back part of your salary. Your employer also
adds their own share. This is not money lost. It is saving that you get later.

[Show: Job A ₹25,000 alone vs Job B ₹23,000 + PF + health insurance]

When you compare jobs, look at salary plus benefits. Benefits are things like
PF, health insurance, and paid leave. A job paying twenty-three thousand rupees
with PF and health insurance may be worth more than a job paying twenty-five
thousand with nothing.

Last point. Freelancers and gig workers must save for their own tax. No employer
takes it out for them.

Let us remember. Tax is charged in slices. A higher rate only touches the top
slice. A raise never leaves you with less. PF is saving, not a loss. Compare jobs
by salary plus benefits. And freelancers must set aside their own tax.

## Lesson 7 - Interest & Growing Savings

### Level 1 - How interest is worked out (about 4 minutes)

Hello! Today we learn about interest. First, a Jataka tale, "The golden swan".

[Show: a swan with golden feathers]

A swan with golden feathers began visiting a poor family. Each time, it left one
golden feather. The family sold the feathers, one by one. For the first time,
they had enough.

But the mother grew impatient. One feather at a time felt too slow. So next time,
she caught the swan and pulled out all its feathers at once.

In her hands, the feathers turned white and ordinary. The swan flew away and
never came back.

The feathers were like interest. The swan was like your savings. If you spend
only the interest, your savings keep making more. If you spend the savings, it
all stops.

So, what is interest?

[Show: "5% = ₹5 for every ₹100"]

The interest rate is written as a percent for one year. Five percent means the
bank adds five rupees for every one hundred rupees you keep.

[Show: ₹1000 + ₹50 = ₹1050]

If you keep one thousand rupees at five percent, after one year you have one
thousand and fifty rupees.

The money you put in is called the principal. The extra money is called
interest.

But interest works the other way too. If you borrow money, you pay interest.

So a higher rate is good when you save. A higher rate is bad when you borrow.

Let us remember. Interest is written as a percent per year. Five percent means
five rupees for every hundred. The money you put in is the principal. Savers
want a high rate. Borrowers want a low rate. And protect your principal, like
the golden swan.

Now try the quiz!

### Level 2 - Compound interest and time (about 6 minutes)

Hello! Today we learn about compound interest - one of the most powerful ideas in
money. First, an Indian legend, "The rice on the chessboard".

[Show: a chessboard with a single grain of rice]

A wise man brought the game of chess to a king. The king loved it. He said,
"Ask for any reward."

The wise man asked for rice. One grain on the first square. Two on the second.
Four on the third. Doubling on every square, all sixty-four squares.

The king laughed. Such a small reward! He agreed at once.

[Show: the piles growing square by square]

The first row was nothing. By square ten, it was a handful. By square twenty-one,
a few sacks. Still easy for a king.

But then it grew very fast. Square thirty-two alone needed about two hundred
fifteen crore grains. And that was only halfway. The whole board needed more rice
than the world grows in nine hundred years. The king could not pay.

For a long time, almost nothing happens. Then the growth becomes huge. Compound
interest works the same way.

So what is the difference between simple and compound interest?

[Show: two side-by-side bars]

Simple interest is paid only on the money you first put in.

Compound interest is paid on your money and on the interest you already earned.
Your interest earns interest.

[Show: ₹1000 → ₹1100 → ₹1210]

One thousand rupees at ten percent becomes eleven hundred after one year. In year
two, you earn interest on eleven hundred, not one thousand. So you get twelve
hundred and ten.

Over a long time, this grows a lot. Ten thousand rupees at eight percent compound
becomes about twenty-one thousand six hundred after ten years.

The biggest lesson: starting early matters more than starting with a lot. Time
does most of the work.

But be careful. Credit cards also use compound interest. If you do not pay, the
amount you owe grows quickly. Next month, you pay interest on the interest.

Let us remember. Simple interest is only on the first amount. Compound interest
is on the interest too. Most growth comes late, so start early. And unpaid
credit card debt grows the same fast way.

Now try the quiz!

### Level 3 - Loans, EMIs and the Rule of 72 (about 6 minutes)

Hello! Today we look at interest from the other side - when you borrow.

A loan is interest working against you. The same compounding that grows your
savings also grows a debt.

Most loans are paid back with an EMI.

[Show: "EMI = Equal Monthly Instalment"]

An EMI is an equal monthly payment. It pays back the loan and the interest
together.

How do you find the real cost of a loan? Multiply the EMI by the number of
months. Then take away what you borrowed.

[Show: ₹950 × 12 = ₹11,400; ₹11,400 - ₹10,000 = ₹1,400]

You borrow ten thousand rupees. You pay nine hundred fifty rupees a month for
twelve months. That is eleven thousand four hundred rupees in total. So the loan
cost you one thousand four hundred rupees.

Now, watch out for the word "flat".

[Show: "flat" vs "reducing balance"]

A flat rate charges interest on the full loan amount the whole time, even as you
pay it back. A reducing balance rate charges only on what you still owe. Two
loans can both say "twelve percent", but the flat one costs much more.

Now, a handy trick. The Rule of seventy-two.

[Show: 72 ÷ rate = years to double]

Divide seventy-two by the yearly rate. The answer is about how many years it
takes money to double.

At eight percent: seventy-two divided by eight is nine. So about nine years.

At six percent: seventy-two divided by six is twelve. About twelve years.

It works for debt too. A loan at twenty-four percent a year, never paid, doubles
in about three years. That is very fast.

Let us remember. A loan is interest working against you. An EMI pays loan and
interest together. Total cost is EMI times months. Flat rates cost more than
reducing balance. And the Rule of seventy-two shows how fast money - or debt -
doubles.

Now try the quiz!

## Lesson 8 - Staying Safe

### Level 1 - Spotting money tricks (about 4 minutes)

Hello! Today we learn how to spot money tricks. First, a story from the
Panchatantra, "The blue jackal".

[Show: a jackal falling into a vat of blue dye]

A jackal was chased through a village. He fell into a big pot of blue dye. He
came out bright blue.

No animal in the forest had ever seen a blue animal. They thought he must be
someone important. The jackal said, "The gods sent me to be your king." They
believed him, and served him.

Then one night, other jackals howled far away. Without thinking, he howled back.
Everyone knew at once. He was just a jackal.

He never proved he was special. He just looked unusual and important. A message
can have a real bank's logo and colours, and still be fake.

So, what is a scam?

A scam is a trick to take your money.

[Show: a phone message - "You won ₹1,00,000! Claim in 10 minutes!"]

Here is a common one. A message says, "You won a prize!" But you never entered
any competition. That is a scam.

Rule number one. Never share your PIN or your OTP. Not with anyone. A real bank
will never ask for it.

Rule number two. Scams try to make you hurry. "Act in ten minutes!" "Last
chance!" When you feel rushed, slow down. Check with an adult you trust.

Rule number three. If an offer sounds too good to be true, it is not true. Nobody
will turn one thousand rupees into five thousand in a week.

Let us remember. A scam is a trick to take your money. Prizes you never entered
are scams. Never share your PIN or OTP. Slow down and check with an adult. And
too good to be true means not true.

Now try the quiz!

### Level 2 - Online money safety (about 6 minutes)

Hello! Today we learn how to stay safe with money online. First, a story from the
Panchatantra, "The three rogues and the goat".

[Show: a man carrying a goat on his shoulders]

A man bought a goat and carried it home on his shoulders. Three thieves wanted
the goat. They made a plan.

The first thief passed him and said, "Why are you carrying a dog?" Then he walked
away. The man looked. It was clearly a goat.

Later, the second thief said, "A good man should not carry a dead dog." The man
started to feel unsure.

Then the third thief said the same thing. Now three people had said it. The man
put the goat down and hurried away. The thieves took the goat.

The goat never changed. Only the number of voices did. Online scams work the same
way. Three messages from three numbers are not three proofs. They are one lie,
sent three times.

Now, online safety.

[Show: a fake email that looks like a bank]

Phishing means a fake message that copies a real company. It can look exactly
like your bank.

Before you tap anything, check two things. Check the sender's address closely.
And check the web address closely. Small spelling changes are a warning sign.

Now, an important UPI rule.

[Show: "PIN = send money only"]

In UPI, you enter your PIN only to send money. Never to receive money.

So if an app says, "Enter your PIN to get your refund," it is a scam. You never
need a PIN to receive.

[Show: "1930" in large numbers]

If something goes wrong, act fast. In India, report fraud on the cybercrime
helpline: one, nine, three, zero. Nineteen thirty. If you clicked a fake link,
tell an adult, change your passwords, and call your bank.

Let us remember. Phishing copies real companies. Check the sender and the web
address. UPI PIN is only for sending money. Asked for a PIN to receive? It is a
scam. And report fraud on nineteen thirty.

Now try the quiz!

### Level 3 - Scams that use your trust (about 6 minutes)

Hello! Today we learn about the most dangerous scams. They do not break into your
phone. They trick you into opening the door yourself.

Scam one: fake helpline numbers.

[Show: search results with a fake "customer care" number]

Criminals put fake customer-care numbers in search results. So only take a
helpline number from the official app or the official website. Never from a
search result.

Scam two: screen sharing.

A fake agent may ask you to install an app, "so I can help you". This is often a
screen-sharing app. Once installed, they can see everything on your screen -
including your OTP. Never install an app because a stranger asks.

Scam three: "digital arrest".

[Show: a video call with a person in a fake police uniform]

Someone in a police uniform appears on a video call. They say you are under
arrest, and you must pay money to clear your name. This is a scam. Real police
never arrest anyone over a video call. Real police never ask for money on a call.

Scam four: SIM swap.

[Show: phone screen saying "No network"]

If your phone suddenly loses network for hours, but other phones nearby are fine,
be careful. Criminals may have moved your number to their own SIM card, so they
get your OTPs. Contact your mobile company at once - by chat or at a store.

Now, the warning signs. Pressure and secrecy.

Scammers say "act now". Scammers say "do not tell anyone". When you hear those
words, stop. Tell a trusted adult.

[Show: "cybercrime.gov.in" and "1930"]

If money is taken, report it fast at cybercrime dot gov dot in, or call nineteen
thirty. The first few hours matter most for getting money back.

Let us remember. Take helpline numbers only from official apps or websites. Never
install an app a stranger asks for. Police never arrest by video call. Sudden "no
network" can mean a SIM swap. Pressure and secrecy are warning signs. And report
fast on nineteen thirty.

Now try the quiz!

## Lesson 9 - Mutual Funds and SIPs

### Level 1 - What a mutual fund actually is (about 4 minutes)

Hello! Today we learn what a mutual fund is.

[Show: many people putting coins into one big pot]

A mutual fund pools money from many people. A fund manager uses this money to
buy shares in many companies.

You own a small slice of the whole pool. So if one company does badly, it does
not sink you. The other companies are still there.

Now, a way to put money into a fund. It is called a SIP.

[Show: "SIP - ₹500 every month"]

A SIP puts a fixed amount in every month. For example, five hundred rupees a
month. You do not need a big amount all at once.

Here is the most important part. Listen carefully.

[Show: "Bank deposit / PPF = promise" vs "Mutual fund = no promise"]

A bank deposit or PPF promises you a number. You know what you will get. A mutual
fund promises nothing at all. It can go up. It can go down.

That is the whole difference. A fund might grow more. But you pay for that with
the chance of losing some money.

Also, a fund that did well last year will not always do well this year. Last
year's result is not a promise.

So when should you use a fund? Money you need soon should not go in a fund. If
you need money in a few months, keep it safe. Money you will not touch for many
years might go in a fund.

Let us remember. A mutual fund pools money from many people. You own a small
slice of many companies. A SIP puts in a fixed amount every month. A fund
promises nothing. And money you need soon should stay safe.

Now try the quiz!

### Level 2 - Risk, time and a fund in your own name (about 6 minutes)

Hello! Today we learn how to choose where to keep money, and how a fund works in
your own name.

Every place for money trades three things.

[Show: three icons - growth, risk, speed]

One: how much it might grow. Two: how likely you are to lose some. Three: how
fast you can get it back.

Let us compare.

[Show a table: Savings account / PPF / Fund]

A savings account is fast and safe, at about four percent. PPF is locked for many
years, at about seven percent. A fund could beat both - or lose money. Nothing is
promised.

But remember, prices rise about six percent a year. So money sitting at four
percent is slowly shrinking in what it can buy. Safety has a cost too.

Now, a SIP. Five hundred rupees a month is six thousand rupees a year. But its
value is whatever your units are worth on the day you sell. That may be more or
less than you paid.

Can a young person have a fund? Yes.

[Show: a child's name on a fund, with an adult beside them]

A minor can hold a mutual fund. The child is the only owner. An adult only helps
run it. Joint holding is not allowed.

Two things to know. First, on your eighteenth birthday the fund pauses. You must
change it into an adult account yourself before you can use it. Second, money
taken out is paid only into a bank account in your own name.

So here is the right order.

[Show: 1. Bank account → 2. Emergency savings → 3. Fund]

First, a bank account. Second, emergency savings. Third, the fund. In that order.
Never the other way round.

Let us remember. Every choice trades growth, risk and speed. Safe money can still
lose value to rising prices. A fund promises nothing. A minor can own a fund, but
it pauses at eighteen. And the order is: account, emergency savings, then fund.

Now try the quiz!

### Level 3 - Spreading risk, and what a fund costs (about 6 minutes)

Hello! Today we learn how to spread risk, and what a fund really costs.

First, diversification. It means not putting all your money in one place.

[Show: eggs in one basket vs eggs in many baskets]

Ravi puts ten thousand rupees into one company. Meena puts ten thousand rupees
into a fund with fifty companies. If one company fails, Ravi is hurt badly. Meena
is hurt only a little. The other companies still stand.

A mutual fund already spreads your money across many companies. An index fund is
a simple type. It just buys every company in a market index.

Now, a word you will see a lot: NAV.

[Show: "NAV = price of one unit today"]

NAV means net asset value. It is the price of one unit of the fund on a given
day.

Next, the cost. Every fund charges a yearly fee. It is called the expense ratio.

[Show: "1% every year, on everything"]

A one percent fee sounds small. But it is taken every year, on all the money you
hold. Over many years, it adds up. Lower fees leave more for you.

Remember: past returns do not promise future returns. A fund that did well last
year can fall this year.

Now, match the risk to the time.

[Show: "Need it in under a year → safe. Need it in 10 years → can take more risk."]

Money you need within a year should be somewhere safe. If you need twenty
thousand rupees for college fees in eight months, keep it safe. Money for ten
years away can take more risk, for more growth.

Last, a warning. If anyone promises fixed, high returns from a market-linked
fund, they are not being honest. Market-linked means it can go down. Nobody can
guarantee twenty percent a year.

Let us remember. Spread your money to spread risk. NAV is the price of one unit.
The expense ratio is a yearly fee. Past results are not promises. Match risk to
time. And no honest person guarantees market returns.

Now try the quiz!

## Lesson 10 - Your UDID Card and What It Unlocks

### Level 1 - The card, the hearing aid and the pension (about 5 minutes)

Hello! Today we learn about the UDID card, and the help it can unlock for you and
your family.

[Show: a UDID card]

The UDID card is the gateway. Almost every other scheme asks for it first.
Nothing else starts until you have one.

To get a UDID card, you need a disability certificate from a government medical
board. It usually needs to show forty percent or more. The law calls this a
benchmark disability.

So, what can the card help with?

First: equipment.

[Show: a hearing aid]

ADIP is the scheme that pays for equipment - hearing aids, and cochlear implants
for people who qualify. It depends on family income. It runs through government
hospitals, ALIMCO, and approved NGOs.

Second: a monthly pension.

Most states pay a monthly disability pension. The amount is different in every
state. It usually needs forty percent disability, plus income and residence
rules.

For families below the poverty line, there is also a central pension called
IGNDPS. Some states add more on top.

Third: travel.

[Show: a train and a bus]

Indian Railways and many state buses give lower fares to disabled passengers.

Now the most important point of today. Listen carefully.

None of this arrives on its own. Somebody has to apply. Even if you qualify, and
have all the papers, nothing happens until you apply.

Let us remember. The UDID card comes first. You need a disability certificate,
usually forty percent or more. ADIP helps pay for hearing aids. States pay a
monthly pension. Travel costs less. And nothing comes unless you apply.

### Level 2 - Claiming it: tax, health and the paperwork (about 6 minutes)

Hello! Today we learn how to claim the help you are owed - in tax, in health, and
in paperwork.

[Show: figures to check against content/schemes.json before recording]

First, tax. There are two sections of the tax law to know.

[Show: "Section 80U"]

Section 80U is for a person with a disability. It lets them take a flat amount
off their taxable income. Seventy-five thousand rupees for forty to seventy-nine
percent disability. One lakh twenty-five thousand rupees where the disability is
severe. No bills or receipts are needed.

[Show: "Section 80DD"]

Section 80DD is for a family member who supports a dependant with a disability.
For example, a father who supports his Deaf daughter, who has no income. He
claims under 80DD. One household usually claims one or the other - not both for
the same person.

Over a working life, these deductions are worth a lot of money. And they are
among the most often missed. Usually not because they are hard, but because
people do not know about them.

Next, health.

Eligible families may be covered by Ayushman Bharat or a state health scheme.
These pay for hospital treatment.

Some support also comes through National Trust registered organisations - like
caregiver help and rehabilitation. But it depends on the disability category, so
check first.

Now, the order of the paperwork.

[Show: Disability certificate → UDID → each scheme]

First, the disability certificate. Then, the UDID card. Then, apply for each
scheme separately. Skipping a step usually means the application gets stuck.

Rules, amounts and income limits change. Always check the official portal or
the district social welfare office before you count on a figure.

And remember: none of this is charity. It exists because being Deaf costs money
that hearing people never have to spend. It is your right.

Let us remember. Section 80U is for the person. Section 80DD is for the family
member supporting them. No receipts are needed. Health schemes can pay hospital
costs. The order is certificate, UDID, then schemes. And check the latest rules.

### Level 3 - Keeping your certificate and your rights working (about 6 minutes)

Hello! Today we learn how to keep your certificate and your rights working for
you.

[Show: a disability certificate with the percentage highlighted]

Your disability certificate shows a percentage. That number decides which
schemes you can get. So check that it is right.

What if you think it is wrong? Say it shows thirty-five percent, but a doctor you
trust says it should be higher. Many schemes need forty percent.

You do not have to accept it. You can ask for a reassessment. Or you can appeal
to the appellate authority your state has set up.

Next, dates.

[Show: an expiry date circled]

Some certificates are temporary. They must be renewed. Check the expiry date on
your certificate, and on your UDID card. Put it somewhere you will see it.

Now, keep your papers safe.

[Show: one folder with certificate, UDID card, income certificate, bank passbook]

Keep copies of every document in one folder. Your certificate. Your UDID card.
Your income certificate. Your bank passbook. And keep a safe digital copy too.

Each scheme asks for different papers. One missing paper can hold up your money
for months. So get everything ready before you apply.

Now, your rights at school and work.

In exams and at work, you can ask for reasonable accommodations. For example, an
interpreter, or written instructions instead of spoken ones. The Rights of
Persons with Disabilities Act, 2016 is behind you.

Last, a warning.

[Show: "UDID - free on the official portal"]

You do not need an agent who charges money to "get" your UDID card. If someone
offers to "fast-track" it for two thousand rupees, say no. Applying on the
official portal is free.

Let us remember. Check the percentage on your certificate. If it is wrong, ask
for a reassessment or appeal. Watch the expiry dates. Keep all papers in one
folder. Ask for accommodations in exams and work. And UDID is free - never pay
an agent.

### Level 3 - Applying well: deadlines, rejections and agents (about 6 minutes)

Hello! Today we learn how to apply well, so you actually get the money.

A scheme is only worth something if your application succeeds. Most failures are
because of paperwork, not because the person did not qualify.

National scholarships are applied for on the National Scholarship Portal. Each
scholarship has an opening date and a closing date. Late forms are not accepted.

[Show: a calendar with the closing date circled]

So before you start, make a checklist.

[Show the checklist on screen]

Income certificate. Disability certificate. UDID card. Marksheets. Aadhaar. And
bank details in your own name.

What if your application is rejected? Do not give up. Read the reason. Many can
be fixed and sent again. For example, if the income certificate was missing, add
it and send again. You can also use the portal's grievance system.

Now, a warning.

[Show: "Applying is FREE"]

Applying for government scholarships is free. Anyone who asks for money to
apply, or promises to "guarantee" approval, is not genuine.

Next, compare options by their total value, not the first number you see.

[Show: A: ₹12,000 once vs B: ₹5,000 × 4 years = ₹20,000]

Scholarship A pays twelve thousand rupees once. Scholarship B pays five thousand
rupees a year for four years. That is twenty thousand rupees. B is worth more,
even though its first number is smaller.

Last tip. Keep a record of every application. Write down the date, the reference
number, and the status.

[Show: a notebook with "Date / Reference number / Status"]

The reference number is your proof. If something goes missing, you can show
exactly what you sent and when.

Let us remember. Most failures are paperwork. Know the deadline. Make a checklist
first. If rejected, read the reason and try again. Applying is free. Compare
total value over time. And always keep your reference number.
